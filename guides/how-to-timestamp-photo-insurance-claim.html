How to timestamp a photo for an insurance claim

Updated 5 October 2026 · 5 min read

When you file a claim, the adjuster is not deciding whether the damage exists. They are deciding whether your photo is trustworthy. The date, the place and the fact that the image has not been altered all matter as much as the damage itself.

Why a timestamped photo matters

A plain phone photo carries its date and location in metadata called EXIF. That metadata is fragile. It is stripped when an image is sent through many messaging apps, cropped, screenshotted or exported for the web. It can also be edited with free tools. An adjuster cannot rely on metadata alone, which is why claims get delayed or denied.

A timestamped photo fixes the information into the image itself. The date, time, coordinates and heading are drawn into the pixels, so they survive sharing and cannot be quietly removed without visibly altering the picture.

What adjusters actually look for

  • A visible date and time on the image, consistent across every photo.
  • Location, ideally coordinates and a place reference.
  • Images that have not been edited after capture.
  • A clear set of shots that tells a story: overview, mid, close up, and any serial numbers or plates.

Step by step

  1. Set your clock to automatic. On iPhone, open Settings, General, Date and Time, and turn on Set Automatically.
  2. Capture with a stamp. Use an app that burns the date, time and location into the photo rather than relying on metadata.
  3. Verify the time. If the app supports a network time (NTP) check, turn it on so the stamp reflects network time, not a clock that could have been changed.
  4. Shoot a consistent set. Wide shot for context, a mid shot, then close ups of the specific damage, plus any make, model or serial numbers.
  5. Add a short note. One line of context, for example the claim reference or the room, saves a follow up email.
  6. Export a report. Generate a single PDF with a cover sheet and the photos, so everything is filed as one document.

Common mistakes

  • Editing or cropping photos after capture, which removes metadata and looks like tampering.
  • Sending originals through an app that strips the location.
  • Inconsistent timestamps because the phone clock was set manually.
  • Only close up shots, with nothing to show where the damage is.
